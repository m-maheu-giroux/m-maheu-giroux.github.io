Real-Time Monitoring of COVID-19 Transmission in Québec

Mathieu Maheu-Giroux, David Buckeridge, and the McGill COVID-19 Modeling Group
Members of the McGill COVID-19 Modeling Group: N Basta, M Brisson, D Douwes-Schoultz, A Godin, D Panagiotoglou, AM Schmidt, Y Shen, Y Xia. Collaborator: M Brisson (Université Laval).

Summary

Epidemic intelligence is the cornerstone of outbreak responses. Without reliable and up-to-date information on the current state of the COVID-19 epidemic, public health authorities are in the dark regarding what type of interventions to implement and (critically) their timing. Surveillance is extremely difficult to conduct during outbreaks because testing is often limited, eligibility criteria for testing evolve, and a substantial fraction of SARS-CoV-2 infected individuals are asymptomatic or with mild symptoms. The overall objective of this project is to better understand short-term transmission dynamics of COVID-19 and to evaluate the impact of mitigation strategies using statistical and mathematical models.

Results

Over the short term, the number of new hospitalisations is expected to increase (Figure 1A). The physical distancing measures implemented so far have greatly decreased transmission. However, the reproductive number (i.e., the average number of transmissions per infected individual) remains above 1 for the moment but the lower bound of the uncertainty interval is close to this treshold value (Figure 1B).


Limitations

These results should be interpreted considering several limitations. These are described below: - Due to delays in the aggregation of hospitalization datasets (Figure 2A), the last 2 days of time series for the number of new hospitalization are censored. - The temporal series are still relatively short and it might be necessary to add further inflexion points to the R(t) curve. - It is difficult to stratify the hospitalizations data (and other databases) by region at the moment. - Some of the parameters have yet to be informed by local data and are based on the observed natural history of the disease reported elsewhere (e.g., time between symptoms onset and hospitalizations). - The model reflects transmission in the general community and does not account for the ongoing outbreaks in long-term-care facilities, where individuals do not necessarily become hospitalized prior to death. Outbreaks in these facilities are epidemiologically distinct with often explosive growth and high mortality. - These results will update daily.

Methods

A deterministic SEIR-type of compartmental model of SARS-CoV-2 transmission was developed based on similar framework, adapted to the idiosyncrasies of the local surveillance data, and coded in R with a C++ back-end, allowing for efficient computations. The model is semi-mechanistic in the sense that it does not explicitly model interventions. Rather, the impact of those intervention is captured by allowing the reproduction number R(t) (i.e., average number of successful transmissions per infected person) to vary with time. The model is calibrated using Bayesian methods, allowing for efficient propagation of uncertainty. It can estimate the degree to which historical interventions decreased the incidence of cases, hospitalization, and deaths.

In the model (Figure 2), the population is divided into four broad groups: 1) susceptible, 2) exposed (but not yet infectious), 3) infectious, and 4) removed (i.e., isolated, immune, dead). The duration of the incubation and infectious periods are both assumed to follow and Erlang-2 distribution.


The model is calibrated to the daily number of new hospitalizations in a Bayesian framework that uses a Negative Binomial likelihood to account for over-dispersion. In Quebec, because of important changes over time in testing criteria, confirmation delays in reporting positive cases, COVID-19 testing efforts, and case definition (contacts of confirmed cases are no longer tested) the model is not currently fit to the number of daily confirmed cases. In addition, as the model attempts to measure overall transmission dynamics in the community, we are not currently calibrating the model to deaths given that approximately 3 out of 4 deaths have occurred in a long-term care facility (CHLSD or RPA). As such, deaths are highly affected by a relatively small number of highly active outbreaks in these establishments with extremely vulnerable populations.
